# Systems Teardown: Kingdom Come: Deliverance 2 and Red Dead Redemption 2, and What Transfers to a Solo AI-Authored Crime Sim (LEDGER)

## TL;DR
- The parts of KCD2 and RDR2 that made worlds feel alive are two separable things: cheap, transferable systems logic (schedules, witness-perception state machines, reputation counters, LOD simulation of thousands of NPCs, need/friction loops) versus expensive, non-transferable authored content mass. Per Dan Houser's October 2018 Vulture interview "The Making of Red Dead Redemption 2," RDR2 shipped "500,000 lines of dialogue" and "2,200 days of motion-capture work, compared with just five for Grand Theft Auto III, requiring 1,200 actors, all SAG-AFTRA, 700 of them with dialogue," plus roughly 300,000 animations; per Warhorse's official X account (13 January 2025), KCD2's script runs "over 2.2 million words." LEDGER should clone the first category outright and treat the second as the exact arbitrage the LLM pipeline exists to exploit.
- Neither game has real per-NPC memory: RDR2 fakes it with scripted flags and short-lived witness bubbles, and KCD2 fakes it with a reputation number plus a witness list that is wiped when you pay your fine. LEDGER's permanent per-character memory plus gossip-through-schedule-intersection is a genuine capability neither AAA game attempted, and it slots directly into KCD2's witness/conspicuousness model and RDR2's witness-chain model.
- The single biggest failure mode to avoid is the "uncanny valley of routine": both games break in visible ways (KCD2 NPCs walking into trees and psychic crime detection; RDR2 NPCs on rails and canned bark repetition). A solo dev cannot out-animate Rockstar, so LEDGER must win on consequence depth and conversational non-repetition, the two axes where LLM tooling is strong and where both benchmark games are weakest.

## Key Findings

1. KCD2 simulates nearly 2,400 NPCs at a 60 FPS target using an AI level-of-detail (LOD) system, up from roughly 550 persistent characters in KCD1, with about half the population concentrated in the city of Kuttenberg. This is a systems achievement, not a content-mass achievement, and the technique transfers.
2. KCD2's crime system is witness-and-deduction based, not direct-observation based, and is governed by a "Conspicuousness" stat (0 to 100, the mean of equipped items' conspicuousness values). This is the closest shipped analogue to LEDGER's seven-slot perception model, and it is cheap systems code.
3. RDR2's living world is overwhelmingly a content-mass artifact: 500,000 lines of dialogue, roughly 300,000 animations, 1,200 actors (700 with dialogue), and recording sessions spanning 2,200 days. These numbers are the definition of what a solo dev cannot reproduce by hand and must generate.
4. Both games fake persistence. RDR2 uses witness bubbles that expire and scripted "remember" flags; KCD2 wipes your known felonies the moment you accept punishment. LEDGER's permanent memory is a real differentiator.
5. The clearest cautionary tales for a generated-NPC world are Watch Dogs: Legion's Census system (procedural NPCs widely criticized as hollow) and STALKER 2's A-Life 2.0 (marketed as persistent simulation, shipped as a spawn-bubble). LEDGER's authored-not-procedural stance plus real memory is the correct response to both failures.

## Details

### PART A: KINGDOM COME: DELIVERANCE 2

#### A1. NPC simulation and daily schedules
KCD2 runs on a heavily modified CryEngine (CryEngine 3.8 lineage) and simulates nearly 2,400 NPCs across two maps (terrain sizes of 2048x2048 m and 4096x4096 m), with around half of the population concentrated in Kuttenberg. This is confirmed in Warhorse's own GDC session abstract for "Supporting Thousands of NPCs in 'Kingdom Come: Deliverance' & 'Kingdom Come: Deliverance II'" (speaker Petr Smrcek, AI Programmer, Warhorse Studios), which states the game "quadruples the number of NPCs on the map to nearly 2400 but also concentrates around half of that into a single city." The stated engineering takeaway is that "it is possible to simulate thousands of complex NPCs in an open world game and run 60 FPS" if limits are set early, and the key techniques named are thread usage, moving standardized logic into code, and knowing what can be stripped away per use case via AI level-of-detail (LOD).

The behavior architecture combines Goal Oriented Action Planning (GOAP) with Modular Behavior Trees (MBTs), presented by AI programmer Matej Marko at the GDC 2025 Game AI Summit. In this model, MBTs specify a desired NPC state and GOAP finds the action sequence to reach it, so designers describe wanted results rather than step-by-step behavior. This lineage traces to Warhorse's "behavior objects" work from KCD1 (Cerny, Plch, Marko, Gemrot, Ondracek, Brom). A behavior-tree component called "Long Distance Move" handles travel between home, workplace, and tavern; Marko's now-famous anecdote is that the system supports horse-based travel but only two NPCs in the entire game actually own a horse, illustrating the "if you implement a feature you have to fully support it" discipline. NPCs continue daily routines and react to player actions even when the player is long gone. Schedules appear to be authored via per-NPC daycycle data (community modders reference an npc_daycycles.xml schedule definition plus AI behavior-tree paths), indicating archetype behavior trees plus per-NPC daily schedules, though the exact authoring toolchain has not been described by Warhorse in text.

Known limitation: reviewers and players report NPCs getting stuck in walls or walking into trees, an acknowledged cost of the flexible AI.

#### A2. Crime, law and reputation
KCD2's crime model is the single most relevant shipped system for LEDGER. Key mechanics:
1. Crime is witness-and-deduction based. Guards can charge Henry if a resident merely spotted him near a scene, and NPCs may deduce Henry is responsible for missing inventory even without seeing the act. A murder with no surviving witnesses can go unpunished.
2. Identification is governed by "Conspicuousness," a derived stat equal to the mean of equipped items' conspicuousness values on a 0 to 100 scale. Plain clothing lowers it; bright, expensive, bloodied, or stolen clothing raises it, as do running, drawing weapons, or being drunk. Low conspicuousness means guards get no usable description.
3. Witnesses persist: if you return to a crime scene, guards may still pursue you, indicated by the punishment icon reappearing. New in KCD2, a witness can either report Henry or extort him for silence.
4. Punishment escalates: fine, jail, stocks/caning, branding, and finally execution. A first serious crime such as murder results in branding with a hot iron, which cuts positive reputation gains by 50 percent, blocks most trade and dialogue except with fellow vagabonds, and can even open access to less reputable NPCs. Committing another serious crime while the brand is fresh can trigger a game-over execution.
5. Reputation is tracked per settlement and per community, checkable in the menu, and remediable via paying indulgences or a penitential pilgrimage imposed by a priest.

Critical failure mode and exploit: the moment Henry accepts punishment, all known felonies in that location are deleted, which is exactly the fake-persistence LEDGER should not inherit. Players also complain NPCs feel "psychic" because the deduction system infers culpability without visible line-of-sight, and the wanted status simply expires over a few in-game days. There is also a smell-propagation system: senior designer Ondrej Bittner described a body-odour debuff that broadcasts a detection circle around a dirty Henry, alerting nearby NPCs during stealth.

#### A3. Needs and friction as immersion
KCD1 and KCD2 use needs and friction deliberately: Henry must eat (food spoils over time), sleep, and bathe; dirt, blood, and clothing condition change his appearance and NPC reactions; weapons and armor degrade and need repair. Social status is legible to NPCs: appear in polished armor and villagers show respect, appear in rags or covered in blood and they treat you as a beggar or a threat. Speech checks depend not only on the stat but on clothing and prior actions, so telling someone you are a scribe while armored and bloodied fails.

The Saviour Schnapps save system is the emblematic friction mechanic. At KCD1 launch there was no free manual save; you had to drink a craftable Saviour Schnapps or sleep in a bed to save, which was highly controversial and later softened by a patched "Save and Exit" option. KCD2 keeps Saviour Schnapps as the manual-save item but ships with Save and Exit from the start, plus autosaves after dialogue and fights. The design intent is that decisions carry weight because you cannot trivially save-scum; the community response was split, with a substantial faction using quit-to-save or mods to bypass it.

#### A4. Dialogue and quest design
KCD2's script is over 2.2 million words. Per Warhorse Studios' official X account (13 January 2025), "Actually, it's over 2.2 million words in #KCD2," reaffirming a figure director Daniel Vavra first gave in August 2024. Vavra's own X post quantified it: "So guess how big the script is? 2,200,000 words! Or 11,000 typical screenwriting pages. That's a 100 scripts for a typical two-hour movie, or about 25 average novels." Vavra added that this makes it "the longest script for [any] game ever, including Baldur's Gate 3" (which holds the current Guinness record at roughly 2 million words). The protagonist's voice actor, Tom McKay, confirmed via a May 2024 Instagram post that he recorded Henry across "508 hours in the studio across 127 sessions," later posting on X "And here's why I was in that sound booth for over 500 hours." He described open-world RPG VO recording as a lonely marathon because every branch of player choice must be covered. KCD2 also has about five hours of cinematic cutscenes (up from around three in KCD1) and used the studio Platige for motion capture, including motion-capturing a real horse.

Quest design uses time-sensitive and self-resolving quests. Some quests are timed with clear in-game warnings ("No Time to Lose"), NPCs express urgency, escort/travel groups leave without you, and some objectives can fail or advance whether you are present or not. The canonical example is "The Jaunt," where two knights leave at dawn without the player who is late or broke, and the quest resolves without them. Compared to KCD1, where quests could expire before you even found them, KCD2 is generally more forgiving: most quests remain available until started, but internal objectives can still fail on a timer.

#### A5. Scope and production reality
Warhorse grew from around 130 developers at the end of KCD1 to roughly 250 by early 2024. KCD1 reportedly cost around 36.5 million; KCD2's budget is commonly cited at roughly 40 to 45 million USD (hundreds of millions of Czech crowns), which the CEO compared to a mid-budget Hollywood film or a Netflix series. Development began mid-2019, shipping 4 February 2025. Warhorse stuck with CryEngine because their internal pipelines are built around it, and added facial capture alongside motion capture and professional stunt riders. The studio's framing is that KCD2 is what KCD1 was always meant to be, now that they have resources and experience, and they explicitly did not use generative AI for writing, dialogue, or voice acting.

### PART B: RED DEAD REDEMPTION 2

#### B1. Ambient dialogue and the greet/antagonize system
RDR2's contextual interaction system lets the player greet, antagonize, defuse, or rob nearly any NPC, with the outcome depending on the NPC's attitude and the player's honor and appearance. The scale is a pure content-mass artifact. Per Dan Houser's October 2018 Vulture interview, the game has "500,000 lines of dialogue" and a 60-hour main story; the final main-story script is "about 2000 pages," some NPCs "have scripts that run for 80 pages," and if all side missions and dialogue were stacked the pile "would be eight feet high." Houser also detailed "2,200 days of motion-capture work, compared with just five for Grand Theft Auto III, requiring 1,200 actors, all SAG-AFTRA, 700 of them with dialogue," with facial reference captured by roughly 60 to 70 cameras. Rockstar deliberately cast talented actors "whose voices you don't recognize" for immersion.

NPCs remember some prior interactions: shopkeepers you robbed or assaulted greet you differently later, and NPCs you rescued in the wild will recognize and reward you in town. However, this is scripted flag memory, not general per-NPC memory, and players note the ambient greet/antagonize lines are generic and repeat, and that antagonizing usually escalates to a wanted state quickly.

#### B2. The honor system
Honor is a single scalar from -8 to +8 (internal value roughly -320 to +320), toggled by good and bad deeds. It gates shop discounts (10 percent at rank 2, 25 percent at rank 4 or 5, 50 percent at ranks 7 to 8), loot quality (low honor yields better tonics and items off bodies), outfit unlocks, some dialogue variants, and the ending. Arthur's honor is capped at the extremes until the story mission "A Fork in the Road." The main critique is that honor is a shallow binary in practice: it self-corrects easily (greeting people and throwing fish back are exploits), rarely changes which events unfold, and high-honor Arthur still voices guilt about killing animals even if the player only hunted for food, exposing the scripted-not-simulated seams.

#### B3. Witnesses and law
The wanted/bounty system is Rockstar's own "Wanted loop." Key mechanics:
1. A crime needs a witness to generate a bounty. The witness runs to report to law; the player can chase and Stop Witness (intimidate), bribe, or kill them.
2. Killing a witness can spawn a witness chain, where the murder creates a new eyewitness, forming a line that eventually reaches a lawman.
3. Identification is binary: "Unknown Suspect" versus "Arthur Morgan." A mask or bandana, or changing clothes/hair, can prevent identification for non-violent crimes, but lawmen who directly spot you identify you through any disguise.
4. Returning to an area with the same clothes, horse, and hair after a prior bounty can get you instantly recognized even masked.
5. Bounties scale by crime and region, cap at $1,500 by the epilogue, and are cleared by paying at a post office. Surrender is available at low levels.

The mask/disguise system and the binary identification are directly relevant to LEDGER's five-rung identification ladder, but RDR2's version is coarse (two states, plus a "lawmen are psychic" complaint) where LEDGER proposes five rungs with recognition gated by relationship.

#### B4. Camp social simulation
The Van der Linde camp is RDR2's social hub and the emotional core. Gang members perform chores, contribute to a tithing/cash box, and hold ambient conversations; the player can do chores, donate, complete item requests, eat Pearson's stew, play minigames, and have one-on-one "therapy session" conversations. Camp morale is a tracked value affected by donations, chores, item requests, lodging upgrades, and decorations; higher morale means members donate more, are more pleasant, and have longer conversations. Members comment on story progress and player behavior (for example, Dutch chides Arthur for loitering "like a bad smell"). The feeling of life is a mix of systemic (morale, chore availability, proximity-based dialogue variants) and heavily scripted (character arcs, set-piece camp events, party sequences). Much of it is optional and missable, which is why it reads as a living community rather than a quest hub.

#### B5. Animation and micro-interaction budget
This is the category a solo dev must not attempt to match by hand. RDR2 shipped roughly 300,000 animations. It uses NaturalMotion's Euphoria physics middleware (also in GTA IV/V and Max Payne 3) for procedural, real-time ragdoll and hit reactions synthesized differently every time, rather than canned death animations. Famous micro-interactions include temperature-reactive horse testicle physics (they shrink in the cold, per Rockstar to Kotaku), context-sensitive skinning/looting animations, weapon inspection, and per-object interactions. Notably, skinning animations can be skipped ("insta-skin") when the carcass is on a slope or against an object, revealing the animation-coverage seams. The honest assessment: Euphoria and the animation count are budget artifacts; the cheap approximations are canned ragdolls plus a small library of reaction animations, and the differentiator LEDGER should chase is reactive text/consequence, not reactive physics.

#### B6. Ambient events and world texture
RDR2 populates the world with a large catalogue of random encounters (ambushes, stranded travelers, snakebite victims, kidnappings, store robberies, rival gangs restricted to their territories, bounty-transport interceptions), most of which can repeat and some of which are character- or bounty-state-restricted. Trains and stagecoaches act as moving, robbable world furniture; wildlife has a predator/prey simulation with fight-or-flight; weather, mud, and snow accumulate on characters; town NPCs open and close shops and populate saloons on schedules; and newspapers report on the player's notable deeds. The systemic frame is thin (trigger conditions plus proximity) and the content mass is what sells it, so the repetition becomes visible over a long playthrough.

#### C13. Where real per-NPC memory changes each technique
Both benchmark games fake persistence, and LEDGER's permanent per-character memory plus gossip changes each faked system into a real one:
1. KCD2 witness list: KCD2 wipes known felonies the instant you accept punishment, and wanted status expires in a few days. With real memory, a witness who saw you would remember permanently, could testify later, and could tell others, turning a transient wanted meter into a durable, socially-spread reputation.
2. KCD2 Conspicuousness: KCD2 collapses identification to one scalar and a binary deduction. LEDGER's seven-slot partial perception (what happened, who, appearance, what they carried, where, when, direction) plus a five-rung ladder means one witness might know "a tall man in a dark coat went east" while another supplies the name only if they already know you, and gossip can merge partial descriptions across NPCs.
3. RDR2 witness bubble: RDR2's witness exists only in a short-lived bubble and the chain resets; the "memory" of shopkeepers is a scripted flag. Real memory means the shopkeeper you robbed tells the neighboring shopkeeper, who warns a third, so the crime propagates through schedule intersections rather than vanishing.
4. RDR2 honor scalar: replacing a single -8 to +8 scalar with remembered events means an NPC's attitude derives from what they personally witnessed or heard, so the same deed can make you feared in one district and admired in another without a global counter.
5. RDR2 camp chatter and KCD2 tavern talk: both are canned loops. LLM conversation with persistent memory lets gang/household members reference your actual last action, last conversation, and current standing, killing the loop.

The techniques that combine especially well with an LLM memory/gossip engine, which neither game has, are: the witness-chain (RDR2) and witness-and-deduction (KCD2) systems, because gossip is literally a witness chain that persists; the greet/antagonize verbs (RDR2), because memory-conditioned generation removes the repetition that players complained about; and per-settlement reputation (KCD2), because gossip through schedule intersections is the natural mechanism by which reputation should spread.

#### C14. The arbitrage: quantified effort both games spent on what LLMs now make cheap
The documented content-mass numbers establish the exact opportunity:
1. RDR2 (per Houser's Vulture interview): 500,000 lines of dialogue; 1,200 actors, 700 recording dialogue; recording across 2,200 mocap days (versus five for GTA III); a roughly 2,000-page main-story script with some NPC scripts running 80 pages; roughly 300,000 animations. This is the effort spent to make NPCs say varied, reactive-sounding things and react physically.
2. KCD2: over 2.2 million words of script (Warhorse/Vavra), which Vavra equates to 11,000 screenplay pages, a hundred two-hour film scripts, or 25 novels; the protagonist actor alone recorded 508 hours across 127 sessions; five hours of cutscenes; a roughly 40 to 45 million USD budget with about 250 staff over roughly five and a half years.

The arbitrage: the majority of that spend bought dialogue variety, barks, and reactive comments, which are precisely what an LLM with per-character memory generates at near-zero marginal cost. LEDGER does not need 700 actors to make 700 NPCs sound distinct; it needs consistent per-character prompts plus memory. The spend LEDGER cannot arbitrage is animation and physics fidelity (item 18), which remains a genuine AAA moat.

#### C15. Known criticisms and failure modes to avoid inheriting
1. KCD2 NPC AI breakage: NPCs stuck in walls or walking into trees; "psychic" crime detection where deduction infers guilt with no visible line of sight. Lesson: make perception legible so the player understands why they were caught.
2. KCD2 fake persistence: felonies wiped on punishment, wanted status auto-expiring. Lesson: LEDGER's permanence is the fix, but it must be counterbalanced with real remediation paths (leaving town, changing appearance, building relationships) or it becomes punishing.
3. RDR2 NPCs on rails: the world is a set of scripted routines that correct themselves back to script; interactions are generic and repeat, and antagonizing over-escalates. Lesson: non-repetition is the win condition for the LLM layer.
4. RDR2 honor shallowness: self-correcting scalar that rarely changes outcomes; high-honor Arthur still voicing hunting guilt. Lesson: derive reactions from memory, not a global morality meter.
5. Uncanny valley of routine (both): over a long playthrough, both worlds reveal their loops. Lesson: the perception/consequence/gossip engine must generate genuinely divergent world-states, or LEDGER inherits the same tell.
6. Procedural-hollowness (Watch Dogs: Legion) and marketed-but-absent simulation. STALKER 2's A-Life 2.0 was marketed as a persistent Zone simulation but shipped broken: per Windows Central (December 2024), GSC Game World acknowledged "many NPCs seem to despawn frequently and won't persist in The Zone if you travel far enough away," and the community "Roadside Panic" modder analysis found NPCs "exist only while in the spawn bubble, once they exit your bubble they are gone for good." Lesson: authored-not-procedural plus verifiably persistent memory is the differentiator; do not overclaim simulation you cannot ship.

## Recommendations

Stage 1 (prototype the core loop, months 0 to 3): Build the perception/consequence engine first, because it is the differentiator and it is cheap systems code. Implement the seven perceivable slots, the five-rung identification ladder with recognition gated by relationship, permanent per-NPC memory, and gossip via schedule intersection. Prove it with text-only NPCs and no art. Benchmark to change the plan: if a witnessed crime does not measurably propagate to at least a second and third NPC through gossip within one in-game week, stop and fix the memory/gossip layer before building anything else.

Stage 2 (systems breadth, months 3 to 9): Clone the transferable KCD2/RDR2 systems from matrix categories (a) and (b): tiered NPC LOD simulation (target a few hundred to a couple of thousand scheduled NPCs), GOAP+MBT behavior, per-NPC daycycle schedules, witness-runs-to-payphone reporting with intercept/bribe/threaten options, per-faction and per-person reputation derived from memory, and need/friction loops. Use the LLM agents to author schedules, encounter skeletons, and reputation-event tables as data. Benchmark: if simulation cannot hold your target NPC count at 60 FPS on your target hardware, cut the count (KCD2's lesson: set limits early), do not cut the per-NPC memory.

Stage 3 (the arbitrage layer, months 6 to 12, overlapping): Wire the LLM conversation system to per-character memory so greet/antagonize/defuse/rob verbs produce non-repeating, memory-conditioned lines, and camp/household/pub chatter references actual recent events. This is where you spend the effort RDR2 spent on 500,000 recorded lines and KCD2 spent on 2.2 million words, at near-zero marginal cost. Benchmark: blind-test whether players can detect line repetition within a two-hour session; if yes, improve memory conditioning and prompt variety.

Stage 4 (avoid the moats, ongoing): Do not attempt Euphoria-class physics or a 300,000-animation library. Buy or license a small animation set, use canned ragdolls, and adopt a presentation style that hides the animation-coverage seams that even Rockstar could not fully close ("insta-skin"). Make the save system optional-hardcore, not default, because solo QA cannot guarantee KCD-level jank is absent and forced friction plus jank is the worst combination.

Thresholds that would change the strategy: If per-NPC memory plus gossip cannot be kept performant at your target NPC count, drop to a smaller "everyone is authored and remembered" town (hundreds, not thousands) rather than abandoning memory; a dense, fully-persistent small town beats a sparse fake-persistent city and is the exact niche Shadows of Doubt validated. If LLM latency or cost per conversation is too high at runtime, precompute and cache memory-conditioned lines offline via the agent pipeline rather than reverting to canned barks.

## Caveats
1. Source reliability: The strongest KCD2 technical facts (2,400 NPCs, 60 FPS target, LOD technique categories, GOAP+MBT, Long Distance Move, the two-horse anecdote) come directly from Warhorse GDC materials and developer quotes. The exact number of LOD tiers, what is stripped at each tier, and hard frame/thread/memory numbers are inside GDC Vault video recordings and could not be verified as text; treat any such specifics as unconfirmed.
2. The 2.2-million-word KCD2 script figure is a Warhorse/Vavra claim not independently verified by Guinness, and some outlets erroneously reported it as "2.2 million lines." The 508 hours / 127 sessions figure is for the protagonist actor only, not the whole cast; total cast size, total recorded lines, aggregate VO hours, and mocap day counts for KCD2 were not found in developer statements.
3. KCD2 budget figures (roughly 40 to 45 million USD) are estimates and community-corroborated CEO comparisons, not an audited disclosure, and are disputed in community discussion as possibly low given headcount and duration.
4. RDR2's 500,000 lines / 1,200 actors / 2,200 mocap days / roughly 300,000 animations come from Dan Houser's October 2018 Vulture interview and are developer-stated but pre-release and not independently audited.
5. Several mechanics descriptions for KCD2 crime/conspicuousness and RDR2 witness behavior are drawn from wikis, guides, and player reports describing observed in-game behavior rather than from developer technical breakdowns; they reflect how the systems behave, not necessarily how they are implemented internally.
6. The STALKER 2 A-Life 2.0 "spawn-bubble" characterization combines a GSC Game World acknowledgment of the despawn problem (via Windows Central) with community reverse-engineering and modder analysis; the modder analysis is credible but not officially confirmed.
7. LEDGER-specific performance and cost projections (NPC counts, latency, caching) are reasoned inferences from the comparators, not measured results from a built system.
