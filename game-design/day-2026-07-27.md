# 27 July — the ending, the third district, and three bugs

You answered six decisions in the morning and said you would test after.
This is what changed, and what it means when you sit down with it.

---

## The one-paragraph version

**Your game has an ending now.** The audit opens off the world's own state,
gives you six days, and resolves into one of five endings without ever
showing you a menu — including for a player who does nothing, which is the
point of a date somebody else wrote down. **Ironside exists**, and it is the
first district built around a mechanic rather than a look: almost nobody
sleeps there, so it is the place where things happen without witnesses.
Building it exposed a traffic bug that had been quietly strangling the city.

---

## 1. Act III — the ledger comes due

You said: *"1 audit"*.

The ending arithmetic had existed since the act was drafted and **nothing in
the game could reach it**. That is now connected.

**How it opens.** Not on a day number. The Table has to have been answered,
and one of the two ledgers has to have become undeniable — either Ossei can
name the rackets, or your operation is simply too big for a bar to explain
its own money. Then a letter arrives on the counter, addressed to the bar
rather than to you, and it names a date six days out.

**Why an audit is frightening.** It cannot be fought, shot, or outrun. And
it reads the one document in this game that has been quietly lying since day
one — where the lie is **wrong in both directions**. Launder too little and
the night money has nowhere to have come from. Launder too much and the bar
earned more than a bar on this street possibly could. You are never shown a
number for this; you are told *"the shape of it is wrong, and a careful
reader will see the shape"*, or, at the far end, *"these books describe a
business that does not exist"*.

**Five moments, fired off what is actually true.** The two worth knowing
about:

- **Lena.** This one waits until you are standing in front of her, because
  the scene *is* her deciding what you have earned. If she barely tolerates
  you she tells you the books are in the cellar and does not offer to walk
  you through them. If she counts you a friend she puts the kettle on —
  which she has not done since Marek died — and takes you through it month by
  month until you know exactly where the lie holds and where it does not.
  **The single most valuable piece of information in the game is gated on a
  relationship.** That is the whole thesis of the project as a mechanic.
- **Succession.** This fires the moment somebody on your crew could
  genuinely carry it: competent, loyal, running something of yours
  unsupervised, and at war with nobody. All facts about how you have treated
  your people. You are shown a name and never a number, and nobody will tell
  you whether they are right.

**Three things you can actually do**, each in front of the person it costs
something with:

| who | what | what it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Halvard** | sell up and pay everyone off | everything, at about half what it cost you |
| **Ossei** | point the audit at somebody else | the person who told you gets burned, by name |
| **your successor** | sign it over | you leave |

**No ending menu, anywhere.** Each ending is a condition the world can be IN
when the books are opened. Several can be live at once, and the last thing
you did decides between them. Do nothing at all and you still get one.

The Quiet Ending — handing it over — is the only one with an **after**:
three mornings where you hear about the street the way anybody who left
hears about anywhere, second-hand and late. What arrives depends on the
world you handed over, not on how the handover felt.

### The hole I found afterwards

Writing the sim gate made me read `Eligible()` again, and **the function that
picks the ending never looked at the books.** Strain was computed, worded,
shown in Lena's scene and printed in the ledger screen — and the audit
resolved without consulting the one document it came to read. Every
laundering decision across three acts was decorative.

Fixed: keeping anything now requires the ledger to survive being looked at.
Managing every mouth on the street does not save books that describe a
business which does not exist — which is the whole reason the crisis is an
audit rather than a raid. Two exemptions, both the price of a door: selling
up outruns the books because there is nothing left in them, and handing over
outruns them because the inspection lands on whoever signed.

### And the audit has a face now

**Tobias Reisz, Board of Excise, nineteen years.** He is at a table in the
bar from nine until six every day until the date on the letter, and he does
not go anywhere else — everybody else in this game has a life you can
intersect, and he has an appointment with your books.

He is not corrupt, and the design depends on it: an inspector with a price
turns the ending matrix into *did you save up*. He is not cruel either, and
that is the frightening part. He explains each step because the procedure
requires him to, he says "of course" when you refuse him, and he is not
interested in you as a person at all.

The only thing about him that moves is **how much he reads**. One item a day
for six days: produce it, or tell him to put it in writing. It costs no
money and it never overrides the matrix — it moves where in the matrix you
are standing. On books that sit near the line it decides the ending outright:
the same world unhandled burns, and handled keeps the kingdom. **Six mornings
of paperwork is the whole difference.**

I picked his name, as with Tomas Vrba. Say if you want another.

## 2. Ironside

You said: *"ironside, rest later"*.

The code had been referring to Ironside as though it existed for a while.
It did not — so a thousand residents "of Ironside" were living on top of the
Hook.

The design doc's brief is three words: *warehouses, logistics, places
without witnesses*. Only the third is a mechanic, and saying it in a street
name does not make it true. Two numbers make it true:

- **34m blocks**, against the Hook's 26 and Copper Row's 20. Fewer corners
  per acre means long walls and nowhere to be standing that is not
  deliberate. Blocks fill with one or two long low sheds instead of a
  terrace of houses.
- **Almost nobody sleeps there.** One person in fourteen lives in Ironside;
  closer to one in three works there. It is busy at noon and empty at
  midnight.

That gap between noon and midnight is the district. **If you work it out,
you have learned something real about where to do things** — and nothing
tells you, which is how it should be.

Two goods roads down off Ironside Road, matching Copper Row's two bridges.
Seven places: the goods yard, the bonded store, the crane wharf, the weigh
office, the night gate, the dry dock, the watchman's hut.

The three districts are now genuinely different in the two ways a map can be
different, both legible from the street without explanation:

| | blocks | who is there |
|---|---|---|
| **Copper Row** | 20m — tightest | dense, all day and all night |
| **the Hook** | 26m | where you live |
| **Ironside** | 34m — widest | one in fourteen sleeps there; one in three works there |

## 3. Three bugs, two of them nasty

**The city's traffic was slowly congealing.** Adding a third district made
it visible: throughput fell by two thirds over three minutes and nine of
fourteen vehicles ended up nose to tail in one corridor, *without ever hard
locking* — which is exactly why nothing caught it.

The cause was that every vehicle picked its destination uniformly from every
junction in the city. With one district that is indistinguishable from
driving locally. With three it made every second journey a long haul across
one of four chokepoints. The chokepoints were doing their job; nobody had a
reason to be anywhere else.

Fixed the way real streets work — most journeys are short and stay in the
neighbourhood, a fifth cross. Throughput is now flat across a run instead of
decaying, and total distance is up 42% on the same seed. The bridges still
carry somebody; they are a crossing now rather than a queue.

The liveness check that missed it took a single snapshot, which cannot tell
a queue from a city dying. It now measures the last minute against the
first.

**The ending never read the books** — described above, under Act III. Found
by re-reading a function while writing a test for something else.

**Act II had no test at all.** All seven of its pressure points have been
wired for a while and nothing proved it — Act I had a gate, Act III got one,
and the middle of the spine had none. I also had a stale line in the roadmap
claiming Act II's moments were not all fired. They are; they were unproven,
which is a different problem. Both fixed.
