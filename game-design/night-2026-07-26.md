# The night of 26 July — what got built

You said: *"router first, economy after. close the gaps you found by
planning/reordering properly and then building."*

All three gaps are closed. Here is what changed and what it means when you
sit down with it.

---

## The one-paragraph version

The game no longer asks you to find a button — you can say what you want to
do and it does it. The street now holds a finite amount of money, so
squeezing it makes it poorer and your bar takes less, which means the
rackets are a real trade rather than free income. The world writes its own
pressure now, from what you have neglected rather than from bad luck. And
Hook Street has three thousand people on it instead of thirty-six.

---

## 1. You can say it instead of clicking it (M6.5, the intent router)

**The gap.** Every action you could take had to be authored *and given a
button*. That is how you build a game when there is no language model in
the loop. With one, the verb *space* can be open while the verb
*implementation* stays closed.

**What it does.** Type anything into a conversation. A fast model decides
whether you are:

- doing something the game already implements — *"how much would it take
  for you to forget you heard that"* runs the same code the **Pay off**
  button runs;
- attempting something the buttons never anticipated but the world can
  still price — *"I buy the room a round"* gets weighed against your money,
  your standing, your crew, the hour, and how much of the street is
  watching, and either lands or tells you plainly why it didn't;
- or just talking, which is most of what you'll say and always the safe
  default.

**Why it is safe.** This is *classification, not adjudication*. The router
picks from a set assembled from live state — literally read off which
buttons are on screen and clickable — and anything it names outside that
set is rejected and becomes speech. Outcomes are still computed by the same
deterministic C# the buttons call. A line of text trying to hijack the
router still cannot reach a verb the game wasn't already offering; that is
tested.

Unambiguous phrasings resolve for free with no model call at all, and that
same path is the whole router when there's no API key.

**Try:** *"Spring was a long time ago, Rocco, and you know what you owe."*

## 2. The street has a finite amount of money in it (M7, the living economy)

**The gap.** The district was a payout table.

**What it does.** Rackets take money out of the street. Wages and generous
cuts put money back. Heat keeps people indoors. Prosperity and prices drift
over a week, never overnight, so you feel a decision before its consequence
lands — and both feed the bar's daily takings.

**So: squeezing the street makes the street poorer, and a poorer street
spends less in your bar.** The racket that pays dirty money at night
quietly costs clean money in the morning, and past a point it costs more
than it pays.

**The lab says that's a genuine trade, not a tax.** Across 900 campaigns,
aggressive play earned **$1697** in racket income over three weeks and
finished **$94 behind** a campaign that ran no rackets at all. No dominant
strategy — which is the only reason this was worth building rather than
adding another income line.

**A campaign that takes nothing is unchanged.** The economy sits at a
takings factor of ~0.98 until you start taking. The week you already know
plays the same.

**Suppliers are people.** Mirek the drayman has brought the drink to this
bar for eleven years, comes Thursdays whether you're awake or not, sells to
eight other places on this street, and knows to the day when he was last
paid. Anton is the wholesaler Mirela's recruitment need has referred to
since the roster was written; he now exists. Both walk their own rounds and
can be talked to.

**Neglect loses a supplier — a poor neighbourhood only makes him dearer.**
The first build lost one in 100% of aggressive campaigns, which is a
scripted event wearing a simulation's clothes: you made no decision and got
a guaranteed outcome. Retuned so paying on time outweighs the worst drift
the street can apply. Now he keeps coming however hard you squeeze — he just
asks for more and doesn't explain the difference.

**No number in this system is ever shown as a number.** Prices rising is
Mirek asking for more. A poorer street is two regulars drinking at home.
The test suite asserts that rather than trusting it.

## 3. The world writes its own pressure (M8, the Director)

**The gap.** The acts' pressure points fire on state rather than dates,
which is good — and they are finite, and they were all written before your
city existed. After the last one, the space between the anchors is empty.

**What it does.** Every few nights, a world-level pass reads the actual
state and schedules the next pressure from it. Five things it may ask for
and nothing else: put a story in the street, arrange for two people to end
up in the same room, have somebody ask you for money by a given day, put
somebody somewhere they wouldn't normally be, or move how somebody feels
about you. Plus **nothing**, which is the correct answer most nights and
which the prompt argues for.

**Pressure comes from neglect, not bad luck.** What it reads first is what
you left undone — a supplier unpaid, a crew member skimmed for weeks, a
debt uncollected, stories in the street unanswered. Inventing a stranger,
an accident or a coincidence is forbidden. That single rule is what
separates a director from a random event table.

**Same guardrail as the router.** Every person it names must exist; every
kind must be one the game has a primitive for; every pressure must justify
itself from something concrete. Otherwise it's a quiet night. Demands cap
at $800 — a demand you couldn't meet is an ending, not a pressure.

**You are never shown what's pending.** You see what you believe, never
ground truth. A panel reading "a demand from Mirek is coming on day 14"
would undo the game.

## 4. Three thousand people (M9, population scale)

**The gap.** The city had 36 people in it. That was never a constraint — we
proved generation works months ago — it was a decision nobody revisited.

**What it does.** Almost nobody is simulated. A resident is a dozen fields
until your attention reaches them:

- **Near** — a walker with a full brain. 22 at a time, on top of the ~36
  authored and generated cast.
- **Mid** — in the gossip mill and nowhere else: carries and passes talk
  without rendering. 110 at a time. This is the band that makes the street
  feel populated by people you haven't met.
- **Far** — a record. Thousands cost nothing.

**The Far band is honest about what it doesn't know.** It answers exactly
one question — roughly what share of the district has heard the talk about
you — and it saturates, because a story never reaches literally everyone.
When somebody is promoted, that share decides whether *this* person had
heard it, by a stable hash rather than a dice roll. Walk away and come
back, and you find the same neighbourhood, not a re-rolled one.

**Anyone load-bearing is never demoted**, and the gossip mill outright
refuses to forget somebody carrying a rumor or a memory. The world must not
lose things because you walked around a corner.

**The whole city saves as a seed plus the exceptions** — a few hundred
bytes rather than three thousand records.

## 5. Four smaller things

**The sounds are connected.** P3 synthesised six of them last night and
almost nothing played them. Now: the low pulse lands on the Fall and on
somebody stepping into your path; the coin plays only when money *actually
moves*, not when you press the button, because a refused payoff should not
sound like one that landed; the bar door plays when you arrive, with enough
hysteresis that it doesn't rattle while you shift your weight on the step;
footsteps come from distance covered, so running is faster steps rather
than the same steps sped up.

**Access is soft keys (M7.5).** Hitman's lesson, which is the best one games
have about doors: a locked door is a wall, and a wall is not a decision.
Nothing here is locked. A gate lists several ways in and *any* of them
works — standing with an organization, how loudly the street is talking
about you (in both directions: some rooms only open to somebody nobody has
heard of, others only to somebody who already is somebody), what you're
wearing, an introduction, money, the hour, something on the man at the
door, how many people you brought. Two rules the code enforces: the
cheapest key you hold wins, so carrying both an introduction and sixty
dollars doesn't silently spend the sixty; and a refusal is a person talking
who names the way in you came *closest* to having, with the figure — a door
you can't open and can't learn about is level geometry, not a system.

**Operation planning (M7.5).** The benchmark scored this 5 against a target
of 75, the largest hole it found. Four choices before a job: approach
(quiet leans on your nerve, forced is reliable and loud, talking your way
in is free until people have heard of you), the hour, who you bring, and
whether you carry tools. Forcing it is *both* likelier to work and much
likelier to be seen — that trade is asserted in the tests, so there's no
dominant approach. The read is words, never a percentage, and it names the
one decision most worth changing. Three outcome bands, and the middle one
is the interesting one: you get most of the way, you leave in a hurry, and
a half-done job is still done. A failure leaves the job there and *harder*.

**CI can no longer waste an hour.** Two compile errors tonight only Unity
could see, each costing a twenty-minute round trip. There are now two
checks that run in under thirty seconds before the Unity install — one for
LINQ used without its `using`, one that builds a real Roslyn compilation
with no references and keeps only the diagnostics that don't need any
(duplicate locals, duplicate members). Both were verified by reintroducing
the actual breaks. The job also has timeouts now; it previously had none,
so a stuck step could have burned six hours.

## What I did not do, and why

- **Wiring for access and operation planning.** Both shipped as Core with
  full test coverage, and neither is reachable from the game yet. That was
  deliberate: four Unity-layer systems already went in tonight and two of
  them broke the build, so I stopped adding Unity code and put the last two
  milestones in as pure Core where the tests actually prove them. Wiring is
  the first job next session and is small — the router will pick both up
  the moment their buttons exist.
- **LLM cost** stays deferred, as you asked. If we publish, the four models
  to weigh are subscription, pay-as-you-go, cheap purchase plus a local
  model, or a dedicated server. `ILlmClient` is a one-method interface
  precisely so none of those is a rewrite.
- **Playtesting.** Skipped per your instruction. Every claim above is from
  the test suites and the balance lab, not from anyone playing it.
