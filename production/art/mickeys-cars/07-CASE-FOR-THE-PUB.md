# The case for the pub, written by the lane that designed the alternative

STATUS: SPEC, written 2026-09-10. Commissioned by name, and with its reason
given: "This is the argument FOR the pub, written honestly, because a package
that only argues its own side is not a decision aid."

## 0. What this file is, and the boundary it stops at

The coordinator ruled on 2026-09-10, filed as `production/queue/245`, that a
second world-designer lane owns WHAT A LICENSED ROOM IS FOR WHEN NOBODY IS
DRINKING, and is writing `production/specs/the-pub-without-drink.md` now. They
have since acknowledged that they commissioned that lane before this file
existed, and ruled that BOTH ARE FINISHED AND BOTH ARE READ, with one named as
the reference rather than the two averaged.

SO THIS FILE IS NOT SUBORDINATE TO THAT ONE AND IT IS ALSO NOT A COPY OF IT.
It answers the same question from a different place. It does not write the
pub's room programme, its room set or its contents, because that is the other
lane's direct answer and duplicating it would waste one of the two. What it
writes instead is the DECISION: what the pub is worth against the alternative,
argued by the lane that built the alternative, which is a thing the other file
cannot do because it has not designed a cab office.

WHAT THIS FILE IS: the COMPARATIVE case. Jafar decides between two premises
from this package, and a decision aid has to argue the option its author did
not design. Every section below is a reason to keep the pub, written by the
lane that spent the day designing the cab office, and the last section is the
list of things that would have to be true for the pub to win.

WHERE THE TWO MEET, AND IT IS THE HINGE OF THE WHOLE DECISION: the strongest
argument for the pub is exactly the question the other lane is answering. IF
`the-pub-without-drink.md` COMES BACK WITH A ROOM PEOPLE HAVE A REASON TO SIT
IN FOR AN HOUR, THE PUB WINS ON SECTION 3 ALONE, and section 6 of this file
turns that into a checklist. Two independent answers to a hard design question
is a better position than one, and this file is written so the two can be read
against each other rather than merged.

## 1. The premise is the pub, and premises are expensive to move

`canon.md` says it twice: line 10 lists "the Hook (old port, the player's
pub)", and line 48 says his uncle "has died and left him the pub, Mickey's, in
the Hook, plus a half-dead criminal outfit and a book of uncollectable debts".

Canon outranks every document and every agent, and changing it is Jafar's alone.
The cab office needs BOTH of those lines edited. That is not an argument
against it, because he can edit them in a minute. It IS an argument that the
burden of proof sits on the new thing, and the new thing has had one day.

And the premise's shape depends on the pub more than one word. A man inherits
a pub, a crew and a book of debts. THE PUB IS THE THING THE CREW USED TO SIT
IN. Nine self-employed drivers who owe the office rent are a good cast, but
they are a DIFFERENT cast from the one Act I to Act III were drafted around,
and `canon.md` OPEN 2 already flags that narrative survival is unsettled. A
change of premises makes that question harder, not easier.

    crime-witness-v1: lines=24 alcoholLines=2 gamblingLines=0 untouched=22/24

THIRTY-FOUR OF FORTY-EIGHT LINES IN THE PUB REGULAR BANK SURVIVE D17 WORD FOR
WORD. That is the pub's best empirical argument and it is worth reading
carefully, because it says D17 does much less damage to the pub than the
inventory of voided decisions suggests.

AND HERE IS THE HONEST OTHER HALF, which the same measurement gives and which
I am obliged to print beside it: of those 34 untouched lines, 31 NAME NO ROOM
FEATURE AT ALL. No stool, no counter, no round, no pumps. Lines like

    Police wagon went up Quay Street twice today. Twice is a pattern, once is
    a Tuesday.

are not pub lines. They are TOWN lines said by a man who is sitting down
somewhere. A man on a bench in a cab office says 31 of them without one word
changed.

SO THE NUMBER PROVES SOMETHING NEITHER SIDE SHOULD OVERCLAIM: the bank's value
was never the drink, and it was never the room either. It is the voice. That
survives both options, and it is a reason not to weigh existing dialogue
heavily in this decision at all.

## 3. What the pub has that the cab office structurally cannot

These are real and they are not reachable by designing the cab office better.

1. A PUB IS A PLACE PEOPLE CHOOSE TO STAY IN. Everybody in a cab office is
   leaving. The waiting room's whole premise is that its occupants have
   somewhere else to be in four minutes, and a four-minute occupant cannot be
   a REGULAR in the sense the moat needs: gossip spreads through schedule
   intersections, and the richest intersection is people who sit in the same
   room for three hours by choice. The drivers' room partly answers this and
   the public room does not answer it at all.
2. A PUB IS THE PLAYER'S FRONT ROOM. Tom lives above the premises in both
   options, but only in one of them is downstairs a place he can sit. The
   emotional geography of an inheritance is stronger when the thing inherited
   is a room you can be in rather than a counter you work behind.
3. A PUB IS INSTANTLY LEGIBLE. Nobody has to be taught what a pub is. A player
   who walks into a 1990 minicab office has to work out what the board is, what
   the screen is for, and why the man is reading addresses out loud. That is
   interesting on the second visit and friction on the first, and the Meridian
   Test's first clause is about the first thirty minutes.
4. A PUB HAS NO INTERFACE PROBLEM. The cab office's best object, the book, is
   only worth what it says, and section 3 of
   `04-HOURS-RETURNS-INTERFACE.md` names an unsolved requirement: a readable
   text surface whose content can change, which the street's decal route
   cannot do today. THE PUB NEEDS NOTHING THAT DOES NOT EXIST.
5. THE PUB IS ALREADY DESIGNED. `atlas-01` authored the plan, the elevations,
   the yard, the sightline study, the five cameras, the hours notice, the
   returns notice and the fascia proof, and a Blender recipe exists for it.
   `atlas-02` authored 334 lines of measured research behind it. The cab office
   has a day's work behind it and no recipe.

## 4. What the cab office does NOT cost, so the comparison is fair

The other side of point 5, stated so this file is not a thumb on the scale in
either direction. Choosing the cab office keeps: the carcass, the two bays, the
six front openings measured at 0.000 mm, the fascia and its cornice, the
private stair with its nineteen risers, THE ENTIRE FLAT ABOVE, the yard, the
1958 WC, and the terrace-end passage. It throws away a counter, a back bar, a
snug screen and a pump clip, NONE OF WHICH IS BUILT. And it refills the beer
store, which D17 voids by name, with a money room that needs no cooling, no
drain, no hose run and no insulated line, which were the four things `atlas-01`
said were unresolved about it.

## 5. Where the pub is genuinely weaker, said once and not laboured

Two things, both of which the pub cannot fix by being better designed.

- THE HOURS. A 1990 pub shuts at eleven with twenty minutes to drink up. The
  studio has already ruled that the last bus goes at half past ten and that
  "the walk home through a dark port town is where the game happens". THE PUB
  IS SHUT FOR THAT. Whatever it becomes without drink, its licence and its
  habits are a daytime-and-evening shape.
- THE RECORD. Section 56 of the 1976 Act requires a private hire operator to
  write down, for every journey, the time, the hirer, the pick-up and the
  destination. A pub keeps no book about its customers because no law asks it
  to. That is one legally-mandated event log versus none, in a game whose moat
  is memory and information.

## 6. What would have to be true for the pub to win, as a checklist

This is the useful form, because it makes the decision testable rather than a
preference.

1. `production/specs/the-pub-without-drink.md` COMES BACK WITH A ROOM PEOPLE
   HAVE A REASON TO SIT IN FOR AN HOUR. That is the whole question. If it does,
   the pub's structural advantages in section 3 all switch on and the cab
   office's advantages become a second location rather than a replacement.
2. The interface gap in section 3.4 is judged expensive, or the answer to card
   2 is that the book is an interface panel rather than a readable object, in
   which case the cab office's best idea gets quieter.
3. The first thirty minutes are weighted heavily. The Meridian Test's clause 1
   is about not bouncing off, and a pub is legible in a second.
4. The narrative baseline in `canon.md` OPEN 2 is confirmed rather than reopened
   in Phase 1 planning. A premise change is cheap while the story is undecided
   and expensive afterwards.

AND THE ONE THING THAT WOULD DECIDE IT THE OTHER WAY: if the answer to point 1
is a room full of people who are there for a reason that has to be explained,
then the pub has become a cab office with worse hours and no book, and Jafar
should take the cab office.

## 7. The recommendation this lane will not make

I designed the cab office and I am not the right judge of whether it should
replace the pub. What this package owes him is both sides at the same
resolution, and section 6 is the honest form of that.

THE ONE THING I WILL SAY, because it is a fact about the two rooms rather than
a preference between them: A PUB IS A ROOM WHERE PEOPLE TALK. A CAB OFFICE IS A
ROOM THAT IS LEGALLY REQUIRED TO WRITE DOWN WHERE EVERYONE WENT. If the game's
moat is social memory, consequence and information, the second sentence is
doing more of the work. Whether that is worth a premise change is his call and
only his.
