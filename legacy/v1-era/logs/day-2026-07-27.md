> **STATUS: LOG, 2026-07-27. NOT CURRENT.** A record of what was true on the

You answered six decisions in the morning and said you would test after.
This is what changed, and what it means when you sit down with it.

---

## The one-paragraph version

**Your game has an ending now.** The audit opens off the world's own state,
gives you six days, and resolves into one of five endings without ever
showing you a menu — including for a player who does nothing, which is the
point of a date somebody else wrote down. It has a face (Tobias Reese, who
cannot be bought and can only be narrowed), a last day you can actually
spend, and its distribution has been measured rather than assumed.
**Ironside exists**, the first district built around a mechanic rather than a
look: almost nobody sleeps there, which is what "places without witnesses"
has to mean if it means anything.

Building those turned up four things that looked fine and were not — the
worst being **a green build that had tested almost none of the game**. That
one is the reason to read section 3.

## 1. Act III — the ledger comes due

You said: *"1 audit"*.

The ending arithmetic had existed since the act was drafted and **nothing in
the game could reach it**. That is now connected.

**How it opens.** Not on a day number. The Table has to have been answered,
and one of the two ledgers has to have become undeniable — either Ellis can
name the rackets, or your operation is simply too big for a bar to explain
its own money. Then a letter arrives on the counter, addressed to the bar
rather than to you, and it names a date six days out.

**Why an audit is frightening.** It cannot be fought, shot, or outrun. And
it reads the one document in this game that has been quietly lying since day
one — where the lie is **wrong in both directions**. Launder too little and
the night money has nowhere to have come from. Launder too much and the bar
earned more than a bar on this street possibly could. You are never shown a
number for this; you are told *"the shape of it is wrong, and a careful
reader will see the shape"*, or, at the far end, *"these books describe a
business that does not exist"*.

**Five moments, fired off what is actually true.** The two worth knowing
about:

- **Lena.** This one waits until you are standing in front of her, because
  the scene *is* her deciding what you have earned. If she barely tolerates
  you she tells you the books are in the cellar and does not offer to walk
  you through them. If she counts you a friend she puts the kettle on —
  which she has not done since Mickey died — and takes you through it month by
  month until you know exactly where the lie holds and where it does not.
  **The single most valuable piece of information in the game is gated on a
  relationship.** That is the whole thesis of the project as a mechanic.
- **Succession.** This fires the moment somebody on your crew could
  genuinely carry it: competent, loyal, running something of yours
  unsupervised, and at war with nobody. All facts about how you have treated
  your people. You are shown a name and never a number, and nobody will tell
  you whether they are right.

**Three things you can actually do**, each in front of the person it costs
something with:

| who | what | what it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Hal** | sell up and pay everyone off | everything, at about half what it cost you |
| **Ellis** | point the audit at somebody else | the person who told you gets burned, by name |
| **your successor** | sign it over | you leave |

**No ending menu, anywhere.** Each ending is a condition the world can be IN
when the books are opened. Several can be live at once, and the last thing
you did decides between them. Do nothing at all and you still get one.

The Quiet Ending — handing it over — is the only one with an **after**:
three mornings where you hear about the street the way anybody who left
hears about anywhere, second-hand and late. What arrives depends on the
world you handed over, not on how the handover felt.

### The hole I found afterwards

Writing the sim gate made me read `Eligible()` again, and **the function that
picks the ending never looked at the books.** Strain was computed, worded,
shown in Lena's scene and printed in the ledger screen — and the audit
resolved without consulting the one document it came to read. Every
laundering decision across three acts was decorative.

Fixed: keeping anything now requires the ledger to survive being looked at.
Managing every mouth on the street does not save books that describe a
business which does not exist — which is the whole reason the crisis is an
audit rather than a raid. Two exemptions, both the price of a door: selling
up outruns the books because there is nothing left in them, and handing over
outruns them because the inspection lands on whoever signed.

### And the audit has a face now

**Tobias Reese, Board of Excise, nineteen years.** He is at a table in the
bar from nine until six every day until the date on the letter, and he does
not go anywhere else — everybody else in this game has a life you can
intersect, and he has an appointment with your books.

He is not corrupt, and the design depends on it: an inspector with a price
turns the ending matrix into *did you save up*. He is not cruel either, and
that is the frightening part. He explains each step because the procedure
requires him to, he says "of course" when you refuse him, and he is not
interested in you as a person at all.

The only thing about him that moves is **how much he reads**. One item a day
for six days: produce it, or tell him to put it in writing. It costs no
money and it never overrides the matrix — it moves where in the matrix you
are standing. On books that sit near the line it decides the ending outright:
the same world unhandled burns, and handled keeps the kingdom. **Six mornings
of paperwork is the whole difference.**

I picked his name, as with Tom Novak. Say if you want another.

### The last day is a scene now

PP5 was the same problem as the inspector, one level down. *"You can reach a
few people, and reaching one is not reaching another. Whoever picks up is the
campaign you actually played."* Good line, describing something that did not
exist — there was nothing to DO on the last day but wait for the date.

Three things you might say, and a budget of **two**, so "reaching one is not
reaching another" is a rule rather than a mood:

- **Lena moves the real books.** The single largest movement in the act, and
  gated on loyalty rather than money — you are asking her to commit a felony
  for you at a few hours' notice. If she says no, it is because she has a
  daughter. That is her reason, not a failure message.
- **Somebody on the crew is told to go quiet.** Out of the count and out of
  the inspection's reach, and whatever you built with them is finished either
  way.
- **Somebody in the day life hears it from you rather than from the street.**
  The only repair this game has ever offered one of those relationships. It
  plants a real *confessed* fact in the mill — they genuinely know now.

All three work down a phone line, which is what makes it a scene rather than
a menu. A phone is a place, so whether you reach anybody at all on the last
day is a question about where they happen to be standing. The M10 exchange
already did that work; nothing new was needed.

## 2. Ironside

You said: *"ironside, rest later"*.

The code had been referring to Ironside as though it existed for a while.
It did not — so a thousand residents "of Ironside" were living on top of the
Hook.

The design doc's brief is three words: *warehouses, logistics, places
without witnesses*. Only the third is a mechanic, and saying it in a street
name does not make it true. Two numbers make it true:

- **34m blocks**, against the Hook's 26 and Copper Row's 20. Fewer corners
  per acre means long walls and nowhere to be standing that is not
  deliberate. Blocks fill with one or two long low sheds instead of a
  terrace of houses.
- **Almost nobody sleeps there.** One person in fourteen lives in Ironside;
  closer to one in three works there. It is busy at noon and empty at
  midnight.

That gap between noon and midnight is the district. **If you work it out,
you have learned something real about where to do things** — and nothing
tells you, which is how it should be.

Two goods roads down off Ironside Road, matching Copper Row's two bridges.
Seven places: the goods yard, the bonded store, the crane wharf, the weigh
office, the night gate, the dry dock, the watchman's hut.

The three districts are now genuinely different in the two ways a map can be
different, both legible from the street without explanation:

| | blocks | who is there |
|---|---|---|
| **Copper Row** | 20m — tightest | dense, all day and all night |
| **the Hook** | 26m | where you live |
| **Ironside** | 34m — widest | one in fourteen sleeps there; one in three works there |

## 3. Bugs — and one thing worse than a bug

**The city's traffic was slowly congealing.** Adding a third district made
it visible: throughput fell by two thirds over three minutes and nine of
fourteen vehicles ended up nose to tail in one corridor, *without ever hard
locking* — which is exactly why nothing caught it.

The cause was that every vehicle picked its destination uniformly from every
junction in the city. With one district that is indistinguishable from
driving locally. With three it made every second journey a long haul across
one of four chokepoints. The chokepoints were doing their job; nobody had a
reason to be anywhere else.

Fixed the way real streets work — most journeys are short and stay in the
neighbourhood, a fifth cross. Throughput is now flat across a run instead of
decaying, and total distance is up 42% on the same seed. The bridges still
carry somebody; they are a crossing now rather than a queue.

The liveness check that missed it took a single snapshot, which cannot tell
a queue from a city dying. It now measures the last minute against the
first.

**The ending never read the books** — described above, under Act III. Found
by re-reading a function while writing a test for something else.

**Act II had no test at all.** All seven of its pressure points have been
wired for a while and nothing proved it — Act I had a gate, Act III got one,
and the middle of the spine had none. I also had a stale line in the roadmap
claiming Act II's moments were not all fired. They are; they were unproven,
which is a different problem. Both fixed.

### The thing worse than a bug: a green build that tested nothing

A build passed today having proven almost none of the game. The bot lost the
week on day six, so the open city never opened, so every gate that guards
itself on `OpenMode` passed *on its own precondition being false* — empire,
Director, operations, Act II, Act III. Nine simulated days, the whole second
half skipped, and CI reported success. **The Act III gate I had written that
morning had never once run.**

I only caught it because I read the numbers under the green tick instead of
the exit code.

A conditional check is worth its tick only if something asserts the condition
was reached. Nothing did. Now the sim forces the city open if day eight
arrives without it — the bot's job is not to *deserve* the open city, it is to
exercise it — and a run that skips the second half is red rather than quietly
green. Losing the week is still reported, because whether a careful player
survives at the current gossip rate is a real question and hiding it here
would have been the second mistake.

Two smaller versions of the same disease, found by looking: the traffic
following-distance check was reporting its "never measured" sentinel and
passing a `>= 0` test, reading like proven clearance. And when the strain
gate went into the endings, **all 24 existing endgame tests passed unchanged**
— because their worlds had no racket income, so strain was zero and the new
behaviour was untested until I wrote 24 more for it. Green tests were not
evidence.

### And a nine-minute typo

A build died on `actTwoOk` where I had declared `act2Ok`. The pre-flight
checker exists to catch exactly that and was throwing the diagnostic away
wholesale, because without engine references every unknown *type* raises it
too. Unresolved types are PascalCase and unresolved locals are camelCase, so
keeping only the lower-case ones catches the typo in under a second and stays
silent on a clean tree. Verified both directions.

## 4. The endings, measured for the first time

`balance-findings-endings.md` — 400 worlds a row, resolving Act III over the
campaigns the balance lab already simulates.

Nobody knew which endings a player actually reaches. Unit tests prove a
*given* world resolves correctly; they say nothing about which worlds a
campaign produces. Three holes:

1. **A player who never built an empire had exactly one ending.** The straight
   life required having *dissolved* an empire — and you cannot sell what you
   never bought. The do-nothing campaign ended in *"you lose the business and
   you lose the people"* a hundred times out of a hundred, having neither.
   Never building it is a way of keeping your life, it is the hardest one to
   play, and it now gets the same door and its own paragraph.
2. **Both fired 51-58%.** You decided it should not be reachable on a first
   playthrough. It was a two-step win button — point the case elsewhere,
   answer the inspector, and an aggressive campaign's strain fell from 1.00 to
   0.39. Handling it well now saves you from losing everything and no longer
   *buys* the best ending: for that the books have to have made sense all
   along. **Now 13%, careful campaigns only.**
3. **A cell that did not exist.** Empire kept, life kept, street managed,
   audit survived — but books saved by handling rather than by adding up —
   qualified for nothing and fell through to losing everything, having
   survived the thing that was supposed to take it.

It reads as designed now: ignoring the audit costs you everything,
stonewalling is worse than ignoring, answering it every morning is the
difference between losing the lot and keeping the street, and keeping *both*
lives is rare and takes a campaign that was careful the whole way through.

## The four gates, and the two bugs behind them — late morning

The coverage floor paid for itself the same day it went in, and not in the way
I expected. With the vacuum drained the sim finally named its failures:

    FAILING GATES: director, ops, witnessCar, coverage

Four gates, two bugs, **and neither bug was in the game.** Both were in the
tests, and both were the same mistake wearing different clothes.

**Three gates were unsatisfiable.** The Director, the operations plan and Act
III all staged on `now.Day >= 9`, and day 9 cannot be reached in a nine-day
run — the Fall moves the calendar three days forward instead of simulating
them, so a fall late on day 8 lands the world on day 11 and the run ends
before hour 11 comes round again. Those three gates had never once been
evaluated in the entire life of the project. They looked green because they
were *also* vacuous: while `OpenMode` was false each passed on its own
precondition being unmet. Draining one hiding place revealed the other.

Staging now keys on the open city *existing* rather than on a date, and the
sim gives back days the clock skips — three days inside is world time, not
simulated time, and counting it as coverage is how "nine simulated days"
quietly became "however many the bot had left". Capped at four, because in the
open city a fall can happen twice and an uncapped reclaim funds its own
extension.

**The fourth was asking the wrong question entirely.** The car gate reads the
gossip mill for a witness who can describe the vehicle — at the *end* of the
run. And `RunTheFall` clears every rumor about the player, deliberately: three
days inside and the street stops guessing because it now knows. So with a fall
in the middle, that gate was asking "did the Fall happen" and answering
truthfully. The car had been described. Something took it away afterwards, on
purpose, and that is one of the better beats in the game.

Latched hourly now, while the run happens.

**The rule grew a second half**, and it earned it three times before lunch:

> A gate about something that HAPPENED must be latched when it happens.

Reading a mutable world at the end and treating the answer as history is a
different failure from the vacuous conditional, and it hides just as well.

### And a correction that cost most of a morning

I spent five builds moving the verdict's print statement around, having
concluded the log channel was truncated beyond use — end of the sim step, a
final step, artifacts, the check-run summary, then log-noise reduction. The
verdict was in the log the whole time. `get_job_logs` with `failed_only=true`
and a `run_id` returns it intact; the per-job `job_id` call returns a ~4KB
tail. I had been using the second one and diagnosing the first.

Two lessons and the second is the one that generalises: a browser was always
the trivial fallback, and **when a channel looks blocked, re-check the
retrieval before rebuilding the sender.**

## What is still thin, and what needs you

**Both decisions answered and built — thank you:**

- **#9, the rackets: coupled.** `Empire.DailyTick` takes a street factor now,
  a starved district pays less, and somebody says why rather than the number
  simply being smaller. The test that pinned the old behaviour was flipped to
  demand the new one.
- **#10, the inspector: relief halved.** `ScopeFactor`'s cooperation term went
  0.09 to 0.045. Stonewalling kept its full 0.15 — the asymmetry is deliberate,
  so being difficult moves him further than cooperating does.

Over 400 worlds a row: the aggressive plan went 100% Kingdom to 100% Burn
Both; cautious-and-answered went 100% Kingdom to a 48/52 split. Cautious rounds
fell 468 to 434 as prosperity dropped to 0.40 — that is decision 9 biting.
Whether it FEELS right is still a playtest question, and both are one-line
dials.

**Still thin regardless:**

- **Act II's pacing.** The seven fire correctly; nobody has watched them space
  out over a long campaign. That is a playtest question and it is yours.
- **Nobody has played the endgame.** It is measured now, which is not the same
  as having been felt.
